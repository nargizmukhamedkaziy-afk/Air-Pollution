Section 1 · The problem

Air that kills, unequally

Air pollution is one of Kazakhstan's largest preventable causes of death, and it hits industrial cities hardest. Fine particles (PM2.5) reach the lungs and bloodstream, causing heart disease, stroke, COPD, respiratory infections, lung cancer, diabetes, preterm births and childhood asthma Kelly et al., 2024.

What the deaths are from

Estimated deaths per year attributable to PM2.5 in 21 cities, by cause Kerimray et al., 2020a. Model estimates, not death certificates.

Heart disease and stroke make up ~70% of the 8,134 deaths: in Kazakhstan, air pollution is above all a cardiovascular problem WHO, 2021. Meeting the WHO guideline could prevent about 1,196–1,698 deaths a year in Almaty and 446–497 in Astana Muratuly et al., 2026.

Who is most exposed

🏭Industrial cities: Pavlodar and Karaganda regions emitted almost half of national emissions in 2025 BNS, 2026a.
🧒Children: 199 died from air-pollution-related causes in 2019 UNICEF, 2023.
🤰Pregnant women: premature birth, low birth weight.
❤️People over 50 and those with chronic disease. Adult cardiovascular disease rose 26% in Karaganda and 1.8× in Temirtau in 2020–25: a trend, not proof of cause Karaganda Sanitary Department, 2026.
🏠Coal-heated districts and homes near plants.

It is getting no better where it matters

In 2025, 11 of 70 monitored settlements had high or very high pollution; Karaganda alone logged over 40,500 exceedances Central Asia Climate Foundation, 2026. Karaganda has been consistently highly polluted since 2021 Kupbayeva, 2026, and Temirtau stays in the “high” category Nosko, 2026d; Birbayeva, 2026.

The social-science question: who gets to define the pollution, and how does that control over information decide who bears its harm?

The Ministry's view

In February 2026 the Ministry of Ecology said the main sources are road transport, private homes and small businesses, not factories Nosko, 2026a.

Independent analysts' view

In Karaganda, Temirtau, Balkhash, Ust-Kamenogorsk and Zhezkazgan, poor air is attributed mainly to Qarmet, Kazzinc and Kazakhmys Smelting Central Asia Climate Foundation, 2025.

Both may be partly right, city by city, but residents cannot check either claim: the data are fragmented or closed. City-wide warnings Sabi, 2025a say nothing about neighbourhoods or what to do. The result is an accountability gap between state-linked emitters and regulators on one side and exposed residents on the other, protecting the stronger party in a monotown bargain of jobs for air. AI is treated here not as a cure but as a possible tool for narrowing that gap.

Where the data are silent: five gaps

1The 2022 break: fewer facilities counted

The new Environmental Code removed category IV facilities from the survey, “one of the factors reducing emissions in 2022” BNS, 2023: part of the fall is a change in who is counted.

2Exclusions that matter for steel

The methodology excludes gases used as raw or semi-finished materials, including carbon monoxide in blast-furnace gas used as process fuel BNS, 2026a. For Temirtau, a blast-furnace steel city, this is not a footnote.

4Thin monitoring where it is most needed

Temirtau has been covered by four Kazhydromet posts, only one automatic Kazhydromet, 2022. Kerimray et al. found no PM2.5 measurements in 5 of 21 cities and only one PM2.5 station in 10, estimating the rest with ±22% error Kerimray et al., 2020a.

5The factory link is closed

Since 2023, older category I facilities must install automated monitoring systems that stream stack data to the Ministry. By March 2024 El.kz, 2024:

Obliged enterprises that had installed the system: 43 of 91

Who falls through the gaps

  • Households at the edge of plants' sanitary zones
  • Workers inside plants and mines: occupational exposure is outside these statistics
  • Small towns and villages beyond the 70 monitored settlements
  • Coal-heated private-house districts, the main PM2.5 source World Bank, 2021, poorly covered by posts
  • Everyone, on health: no public dataset links hospital admissions to pollution episodes

Section 3 · Current state of research

Four ways of studying dirty air, and the gap between them

Four approaches, each answering a different question; none connects knowledge to action.

Health-burden studies

Kerimray et al. made the first multi-city estimate, ranking Temirtau among the three worst cities 2020a; Muratuly et al. added year-long sampling and costing 2026; Agibayeva et al. linked forecasts to years of healthy life lost 2022; CREA modelled the Temirtau plant's toll Kelly et al., 2024. A risk strand found unacceptable risk in most of 26 cities, with a short-term PM2.5 hazard quotient of 38.5 in Karaganda, where 1.0 is safe Kenessary et al., 2019.

Shared weakness: sparse monitoring and uncertain mortality data, and they stop at the estimate. They tell policymakers pollution kills; they create no way to act on it.

Monitoring and source analysis

Almaty's winter peaks are driven by the coal power plant and household stoves Kerimray et al., 2020b; the 2020 lockdown removed traffic but did not clean the air, pointing to heating Kerimray et al., 2020c. The World Bank named residential heating the main national PM2.5 source 2021; see also Zhakiyev et al., 2025.

Shared weakness: almost all about Almaty and Astana, so “small sources dominate” is better evidenced for the capitals than for industrial cities.

Prediction and estimation

In Astana, random forests beat linear regression for PM2.5 forecasts Agibayeva et al., 2022. Globally, AirQo calibrates cheap sensors with machine learning Bainomugisha et al., 2024, and Climate TRACE estimates emissions for about 900 steel plants from satellites Climate TRACE, 2024; Lancellotti et al., 2025.

Shared weakness: accuracy is reported, use is not. Beijing's IBM forecasts (2014–15) were used, but arrived alongside big emission controls, so AI's own effect was never isolated Laursen, 2017.

Citizen science and environmental justice

Pollution falls unevenly, and the most exposed have the least say Mohai et al., 2009. Official standards both lend credibility to residents' own samples and let experts dismiss them Ottinger, 2010; citizen sensor data can be “just good enough” to press for action Gabrys et al., 2016.

Shared weakness: it fits Kazakhstan's industrial cities, but no study has applied it there.

Section 4 · The AI-assisted solution

Taza Aua: warn citizens, hold emitters to account

Three layers, rolled out from the high-pollution cities, built on infrastructure Kazakhstan already has.

What the results showSuccess came where forecasts were tied to action: Delhi's commission uses them to trigger restrictions, AirQo works with the city authority, and Korean alerts changed behaviour. The one failure, Port Talbot, is a steel town where alerts went to individuals with nothing asked of the emitter. It is the closest match to Kazakhstan's metallurgy towns. Kazakhstan's own tools exist but have never been evaluated. The lesson for Taza Aua: accuracy is achievable; impact depends on who is obliged to act.

The proposal: three layers

National · run by Kazhydromet

Machine-learning models forecast PM2.5, NO₂ and SO₂ 24–72 hours ahead for all 70 settlements, combining Kazhydromet readings, weather, satellite data and a global AI forecast such as Aurora. Kazhydromet already runs a Finnish forecasting model for each post Kazhydromet, 2020, 2024; missing are neighbourhood detail and personal warnings, which let people cut exposure in advance World Bank, 2025. Messages are tiered:

  1. Everyone: a daily neighbourhood forecast in AirKZ, eGov and Telegram, in Kazakh and Russian.
  2. Vulnerable people who opt in: an SMS or push message the evening before a bad-air day, with concrete advice. A language-model assistant answers follow-ups only from doctor-reviewed answers and live data.
  3. Institutions: schools move PE indoors; clinics expect more respiratory visits.
  4. Severe episodes only: Mass Alert cell broadcast. Using it rarely protects its credibility.
Cities · run by Kazhydromet

Low-cost sensor networks like Almaty's are extended to the high-pollution cities first, then to other centres. Machine learning calibrates them against official posts, as AirQo does, producing neighbourhood maps with published error margins that also show where coal stoves drive winter peaks, so gasification subsidies can be targeted.

Every industrial city with category I emitters · run by an independent consortium

Combining factories' own stack data, satellites, Climate TRACE and wind, the system flags high readings downwind of a plant or mismatches with reported emissions. Each flag goes to the regional ecology department, whose response is published beside it.

A flag is a request to inspect, not a verdict. Attribution needs dispersion modelling, as CREA and Delhi use. Layer 3 is run by universities, NGOs and residents' councils with guaranteed data access, because the Ministry has already said factories are not the main problem.

AI can also test policies, as in Delhi, for example measuring the effect of Almaty's low-emission-zone pilot Omirgazy, 2026. All of this is useful only if someone must act on the output.

Section 4 · The bill

What it would cost, and who pays

Prices use a real Kazakh benchmark: the Nurlan Smagulov Foundation gave 148 million tenge to buy and install 200 Almaty sensors Forbes.kz, 2025, or 740,000 tenge each.

Against the harm

About USD 0.92 m a year is under 0.01% of the USD 10.8–12.7 bn yearly damage World Bank, 2025. But low cost is not proven benefit: the system is worth its price only if alerts change behaviour, which Port Talbot warns may not happen.

Who pays, who profits

The state funds Layers 1–2 as public health; Layer 3 needs independent university or donor funding. Mobile operators, sensor makers and cloud providers gain. Foreign services such as Google's API would create dependence, so open models hosted in Kazakhstan are preferred.

Why these numbers: 8,760 is the number of hours in a year (24 × 365), because forecasts and sensors run non-stop. 0.5 kW is the upper end for one ordinary server, whose typical draw is about 0.4–0.5 kW Oracle, n.d.. 2 W is a deliberately generous figure for a low-cost sensor, which draws about 1 W PurpleAir, n.d., leaving room for a mobile-data connection and winter operation.

A chatbot generating new text for every question would raise these figures; pre-approved answers keep them low.

Small, but on a coal grid

The models run on ordinary servers, but two-thirds of Kazakhstan's electricity is coal Agora Energiewende, 2024. AI's water footprint includes water used to generate power Li et al., 2023, and the life cycle includes replacing sensors that face −30°C winters Luccioni et al., 2024.

Trade-off: hosting in Kazakhstan protects data control (Condition 5) but ties the system to coal. It should publish its own energy use.

AI depends on hidden human labour Tubaro et al., 2020; Williams & Miceli, 2023. These people must be paid and credited.

Surveillance creep

Almaty plans 100,000 cameras and a single “112” command centre alongside its air sensors Almaty City Akimat, 2026; the digital minister has compared environmental monitoring to Sergek road cameras Daryo, 2024. An alert app holding locations and health status must never be merged with these.

Alert fatigue, unequal reach

People complain “112” SMS arrive late, contradict each other and come too often Mishura, 2026. Mass Alert misses iPhones that cannot run iOS 18 Sabi, 2025c. Older and poorer residents, who are more vulnerable, are most likely to be missed.

Blaming the victims

An alert tells residents to stay in; it does not tell the emitter to stop. Warning-only systems turn pollution into individual precaution, which environmental justice research warns against Mohai et al., 2009. This is why Layer 3 is not optional.

Verdict

Yes — but only under six conditions

Kazakhstan should build Taza Aua, starting with the high-pollution cities. If any condition is missing, it either fails to protect people or warns them without showing where pollution comes from.

1

Accurate before it goes public

Forecasts are tested on past winters in every city, including Karaganda, Temirtau and Ust-Kamenogorsk. Alerts start only when bad-air days are reliably predicted; every forecast shows its uncertainty.

Builds on what exists
2

Open data

Air readings and the emissions data factories already send to the Ministry are published in real time.

Political decision
3

Clear advice, not numbers

“Keep children indoors tomorrow”, in Kazakh and Russian, written with doctors. Opt-in personal alerts; emergency broadcasts for the worst days only.

Builds on what exists
4

People decide, not AI

AI shows inspectors where to look first; it never blames a factory or issues fines. The regional department must publicly respond to every flag within a set time.

Political decision
5

Privacy and independence

Data stored in Kazakhstan; no medical details. Layer 3 run by universities, NGOs and residents; the whole system independently audited, as the AI Law provides.

Political decision
6

Nobody left out

SMS for phones without cell broadcast; schools, clinics and care homes alerted directly. Success = fewer emergency visits for breathing problems on bad-air days, not app downloads.

Builds on what exists
If only Layer 1 is built: noThe money would do more good on automatic reference stations in industrial cities and a legal duty to publish factories' emissions data, neither of which needs AI.
Why the bill makes this a real choiceAt about 479 million tenge a year, roughly nine reference stations, it is worth building only if Layer 3 turns data into inspections, and a pilot proves alerts reduce hospital visits.

Better technology does not automatically produce better governance. It can make it harder for weak governance to hide, if the institutions agree to be held to what the data show.
